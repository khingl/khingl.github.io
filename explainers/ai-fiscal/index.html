How Much AI Growth Will It Take to Grow Us Out of Debt?

Productivity, Longevity, and the U.S. Fiscal Gap

Abstract

Optimists argue that artificial intelligence will raise growth enough to fix the federal budget. This document asks how much faster growth would need to be and compares the answer with published forecasts. Scaling the sensitivity analyses of the Congressional Budget Office and the Social Security Trustees, it finds that holding federal debt near today’s 100 percent of GDP for thirty years would require AI to add about 1.2 percentage points to annual productivity growth. Closing Social Security’s 75-year shortfall would require about 2.3 points. Most forecasts lie between 0.1 and 1.0 points. Longevity pushes the other way. If AI-driven medicine made death rates fall 2 percent a year, twice the pace of the past century, Social Security would lose the full benefit of an extra point of growth. A simple model built on an expert survey puts the odds that AI growth alone stabilizes the debt at about one in five, and the odds that it rescues Social Security at about one in twenty.

1Introduction

In a talk in early November 2026, Tyler Cowen predicted that AI would add about half a percentage point to annual growth in U.S. GDP per capita.The talk was given in Barcelona. This summary follows Henderson (2026), who also proposes giving each retiree a fixed 20-year window of full benefits, to be drawn whenever they choose. That, he argued, would be enough for the government to pay Social Security without cutting benefits. Later in the same talk he suggested that AI-driven medicine could let people now 40 or younger live to 120. Henderson (2026) spotted the tension. Growth helps the budget, but longevity hurts it. A retiree who claims at 70 and lives to 110 or 120 collects benefits for 40 to 50 years instead of about 18.

The hope that growth will rescue the budget is not confined to economists’ talks. “With 3% growth, we grow our way out of this,” Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said in September 2026. A month later, CBO Director Phillip Swagel offered the official rejoinder: “growth will help, but it’s probably not plausible that growth alone will stabilize our fiscal trajectory.”Both quotations as reported by Ma (2026). Swagel added that with interest rates of 4–5 percent, growth alone would have to run at 5–6 percent a year to stabilize the debt.

This document asks how likely it is that AI-driven growth gets the United States out of its fiscal hole, once we allow for the possibility that the same technology lengthens lives. It builds no new macroeconomic model. Instead, it takes the official sensitivity analyses at face value, scales them up, and compares the growth they require with the growth that forecasters expect. The findings are threefold:

  1. The bar is high. To keep federal debt from rising above today’s level through the mid-2050s, AI would need to add about 1.2 points to annual productivity growth, sustained for three decades. To close Social Security’s 75-year gap through growth alone would take about 2.3 points, which means real wages growing roughly 3.4 percent a year for 75 years. The past 20 years averaged about 0.9 percent.
  2. Forecasts fall short. Nearly every central forecast, from Acemoglu (2025)’s 0.1 points to Aghion and Bunel (2024)’s 0.8 to 1.3, lies below the federal threshold, and every one lies below the Social Security threshold. Only the boom scenarios clear them.
  3. Longevity is a large offset. Each extra 0.1 point of annual decline in death rates costs Social Security about as much as 0.08 points of extra growth adds. A decline of 2 percent a year erases a full point of growth.

Putting these together in a simple model whose inputs come from an expert survey, the odds that AI growth alone stabilizes the debt are about one in five. The odds that it closes Social Security’s gap are about one in twenty. Section 6 lets you put in your own assumptions.

Several 2026 studies reach related conclusions from different angles. Harris, Mehrotra, and Overcash (2026) find that a large, conventional productivity shock would cut the 2036 deficit from about 6 to about 2 percent of GDP. Here, they write, “the techno-optimists are validated.” But their AI-specific scenarios keep only about half of that gain, or less. Some assume a smaller boom, and all add offsets: longer lives, a shift of income from labor to capital, or higher interest rates. The Yale Budget Lab (2026) finds that a large, persistent productivity boom would make the fiscal path “more sustainable” even after interest rates rise, while warning that falling labor supply would offset part of the gain. Riedl (2026) puts the savings at perhaps $600 billion a year within a decade, a little over one-eighth of the deficit she projects under current policies. CRFB (2026) calculates that stabilizing the debt would take 2.5 percent TFP growth for a decade, a pace never sustained in the postwar era. This document differs in three ways. It puts growth and longevity on a single map. It looks beyond the 10-year window, to the horizon over which Social Security’s wage-indexed benefits catch up with wages. And it turns the comparison into explicit odds.

2The size of the problem

The fiscal problem has two parts: a deadline and a gap. The deadline is close. The Social Security retirement trust fund runs out in late 2032. From then on, the fund can pay only 78 percent of scheduled retirement benefits, a share that falls to 62 percent by 2100. Medicare’s hospital insurance fund runs out in 2033, and the combined Social Security funds in 2034 (SSA Trustees 2026; Medicare Trustees 2026).

The gap is large. Under CBO’s long-term projections, debt held by the public rises from 101 percent of GDP in 2026 to 175 percent in 2056, well past the 1946 record of 106 percent (Figure 1a).CBO released its 2026 long-term projections as data only. The figures here are from that data file. Over the same period, net interest climbs from 3.3 to 6.9 percent of GDP. Keeping the debt at its 2026 level would require primary deficits that average about 2 percentage points of GDP smaller over thirty years (Swagel 2026). Social Security alone faces a 75-year shortfall of 4.42 percent of taxable payroll, about 1.5 percent of GDP, up from 3.82 percent of payroll a year earlier. Spending on Social Security and Medicare together rises from 9.4 to 14.2 percent of GDP by 2100 (Figure 1b), while Social Security’s own income stays between 4.4 and 4.8 percent.

These projections are not knife-edge. The Social Security Trustees run 5,000 random simulations of the economy and population. In 97.5 percent of them the 75-year deficit is at least 1.86 percent of payroll, and in 97.5 percent the trust funds run dry by 2039. Even the Trustees’ low-cost scenario, in which every assumption breaks favorably at once, still runs out of money in 2048.

3How much growth would it take?

Faster growth helps the budget. But three features of U.S. fiscal law mean it helps less than intuition suggests.

Benefits follow wages. A worker’s starting Social Security benefit is computed from lifetime earnings indexed to economy-wide wage growth. So when AI raises wages, it raises payroll-tax revenue at once, and it also raises the benefits that today’s workers will collect later. The Trustees explain why growth still helps: “Higher wages increase taxable payroll immediately, but they increase benefit levels only gradually,” and benefits after retirement rise only with prices. Still, the offset is large. The 2026 report raised projected real earnings growth for 2025–35 from 1.44 to 1.72 percent a year. Together with the report’s other economic updates, that improved the 75-year balance by only 0.10 percent of payroll, and the combined funds’ depletion date did not move. As Kent Smetters of the Penn Wharton Budget Model put it, a faster productivity boost “barely moves the balance because the initial benefits go up.”As reported in Pringle (2026).

Interest rates rise with growth. In standard models, faster expected growth raises real interest rates (Chow, Halperin, and Mazlish 2026). Furman (2026) reports a simulation in which markets expect the AI buildout to continue through 2031 and productivity growth to rise by half a point. The 10-year Treasury yield rises by about 75 basis points. With debt above 100 percent of GDP, this matters: CBO calculates that interest rates one point higher than projected would push 2056 debt from 175 to 222 percent of GDP (Swagel 2026).

The tax system leans on labor income. If AI’s gains flow mainly to profits, capital, or salaries above the payroll-tax cap, much of the growth never reaches Social Security. Growth in capital income raises only about half as much federal revenue as the same growth in labor income (CRFB 2026). In the most dramatic scenario of Korinek et al. (2026), labor’s share of income falls from 60 to 45 percent by 2030.

With these leaks in mind, the official sensitivity analyses give the thresholds.

Social Security. The Trustees report that each 0.1-point increase in long-run real wage growth improves the 75-year balance by about 0.19 percent of payroll. Extending that slope, Cowen’s half point closes 22 percent of the gap, and Henderson’s full point closes 43 percent. Closing the whole gap takes about 2.3 extra points: real wages would have to grow about 3.4 percent a year for 75 years, compared with the Trustees’ 1.14 percent and roughly 0.9 percent over the past two decades (SSA Trustees 2026, Table V.B1). If only half of AI’s gains reach taxable wages, the requirement doubles to 4.6 points.

Federal debt. CBO’s most recent productivity sensitivity, from May 2025, found that total factor productivity (TFP) growth half a point faster than projected would leave debt at 113 percent of GDP in 2055, against a baseline of 156 percent (CBO 2025). That scenario already lets interest rates rise somewhat. The 2026 baseline is worse, so scaling the same effect implies that holding debt near 101 percent through 2055 would take roughly 0.8 points of extra TFP growth, about 1.2 points of labor-productivity growth.In growth accounting, labor productivity grows by roughly TFP growth divided by labor’s share in production, about 1.5 times TFP once capital accumulates to match. The scaling assumes the debt effect per point of TFP growth is the same in the 2026 baseline as in the 2025 one; because the 2026 baseline starts with more debt, the true effect is probably a little larger.

The federal bar is lower than Social Security’s for two reasons. It sets a modest goal: it stops debt from rising further from a record level, rather than paying every promised benefit for 75 years. And income-tax brackets are indexed to prices, not wages, so real growth pushes revenue up faster than GDP.

4What forecasters expect

Figure 2 sets these thresholds against published estimates of AI’s effect on growth. The forecasts fall into three groups. Skeptics: Acemoglu (2025) estimates a TFP gain of under 0.07 points a year over a decade; CBO builds 0.1 points into its baseline for 2026–36 (CBO 2026a); and the Penn Wharton Budget Model (2025) projects a TFP boost that peaks at 0.2 points in 2032 and then fades. In the middle, estimates from the OECD (Filippucci, Gal, and Schief 2024) and McKinsey (McKinsey Global Institute 2023) run from 0.1 to 0.9 points, and a Dallas Fed illustration uses 0.3 (Wynne and Derr 2025); Cowen’s half point sits here (Cowen 2025). Optimists: Aghion and Bunel (2024) put the boost at 0.8 to 1.3 points over a decade, and Goldman Sachs at just under 1.5 points in the decade after wide adoption (Briggs and Kodnani 2023).

The best evidence on what experts actually believe comes from Karger et al. (2026), who surveyed economists, AI industry and policy professionals, superforecasters, and the general public. The median economist expects 2.5 percent annual GDP growth in the late 2040s, about 0.8 points above the 1.7 percent long-run baseline used by the Trustees and CBO. Only the boom scenarios reach the thresholds. Economists give 14 percent odds to “rapid” AI progress, in which AI surpasses humans at many cognitive and physical tasks by 2030. In that world, the median economist forecasts 3.5 percent growth in the late 2040s, or about 1.8 points of extra growth. That clears the federal line but not the Social Security line. Further out are Dario Amodei’s suggestion that 10–20 percent growth “may be possible” (Amodei 2026) and the “extreme” scenario of Korinek et al. (2026), in which growth reaches 15 percent by 2030.

Figure 2 also flatters the optimists in one respect. Most forecasts describe a decade-long boost, but the thresholds require the boost to last thirty to seventy-five years. A temporary acceleration raises the level of wages for good. Social Security, however, eventually pays a higher wage level back out as higher benefits, so a one-time level gain does little for its 75-year balance. Early data are encouraging but not decisive. Nonfarm productivity grew 3.2 percent in 2024 and 2.3 percent in 2025, against 1.5 percent over 2007–19 (BLS 2026). Productivity data are noisy, however, and it is too early to tell how much of the recent strength comes from AI.

5The longevity offset

Henderson’s point is that AI may lengthen lives as well as raise incomes. The Trustees assume death rates will fall 0.73 percent a year. Over the past century they fell about 1.0 percent a year. Each extra 0.1 point of annual decline worsens the 75-year balance by about 0.15 percent of payroll. Set against 0.19 percent of payroll per 0.1 point of growth, this gives a simple exchange rate: a decline in death rates of about 2 percent a year cancels a full point of extra growth. In more intuitive units, each extra year that a 65-year-old can expect to collect benefits costs about 0.27 percent of payroll, so one point of extra growth pays for roughly seven more years of retirement, and no more.Across the Trustees’ three mortality scenarios, period life expectancy at 65 in 2100 is 21.0, 23.6, and 26.5 years. One point of growth is worth 1.92 percent of payroll; one year of life expectancy at 65 costs about 0.27 percent; 1.92 / 0.27 ≈ 7. Under a 2 percent decline, a 65-year-old in 2100 would expect to live to about 96 rather than the Trustees’ 89, collecting benefits for about 31 years rather than 24.

Figure 3 puts both forces on one map. Moving right is faster wage growth; moving up is a longer expected life for a 65-year-old, which under current law means more years of benefits. Above and to the left of the black line, Social Security runs short. The map shows that growth-only optimism and health optimism pull in opposite directions:

  • Cowen’s and Henderson’s growth figures on their own (+0.5 and +1.0 along the Trustees’ line) leave most of the gap open.
  • The “health” scenario of Harris, Mehrotra, and Overcash (2026), with death rates falling 3 percent a year, leaves Social Security worse off than with no AI at all.
  • The scenario that comes closest to balance, Brookings’ “traditional” shock, is the one that assumes AI does nothing for lifespans.
  • Combining each person’s growth estimate with that person’s own longevity speculation (the rings) lands deep in shortfall. Neither Cowen nor Henderson proposes paying benefits for that long; Henderson’s point is precisely that current law would have to change. Lifespans of 110 to 120 imply 45 to 55 years of benefits under current rules, which would need mortality to fall 4.5 to 6.3 percent a year, and the growth they discuss pays for only a few of those extra years.

How plausible are large longevity gains? The leaders of the AI labs are bullish. Dario Amodei expects AI to “compress” 50–100 years of biology into 5–10 and suggests lifespans could double to 150 (Amodei 2024). Demis Hassabis calls the end of disease “within reach” in a decade or so (CBS News 2025). Demographers are skeptical. Olshansky et al. (2024) find that life-expectancy gains in the longest-lived countries have been slowing since 1990. Curing a single disease does surprisingly little, because people spared one cause of death die of another: eliminating all cancer would raise life expectancy at 65 by about 2.2 years, and eliminating heart disease by about 3.2 (Arias, Heron, and Tejada-Vera 2013). Radical life extension would require slowing aging itself, not curing diseases one at a time. The fiscal risk is correlated, though. The worlds in which AI is capable enough to deliver 2 points of growth are the worlds in which it is most likely to transform biology.

Medicare is exposed differently. When longer lives are healthy lives, as they have been recently, they raise Social Security costs more than Medicare costs. Between 1992 and 2019, changes in elderly health and mortality raised expected lifetime Social Security spending by 14 percent but Medicare spending by only 6 percent (Einav and Finkelstein 2026). Medicare’s main risk is health-cost growth, and here AI could help directly. Under current law, payment updates for most non-physician providers are reduced by a ten-year average of economy-wide productivity growth, so an AI boom trims Medicare prices automatically, though with a lag. The catch, which the Medicare Trustees flag, is whether providers can sustain those cuts.

6How likely is it?

To turn the comparison into odds, this document uses a deliberately simple model whose every input you can change below. It starts from the scenario probabilities in Karger et al. (2026): economists put 39 percent on slow AI progress, 47 percent on moderate progress, and 14 percent on rapid progress. Within each scenario s, sustained extra productivity growth g is normally distributed with mean μs and standard deviation σs, and death rates fall at rate ms. The odds of clearing a bar are

Pr(clear)  =  ∑s ps · Pr( λ g ≥ T(ms)  |  s ),

where T(m) is the threshold from Sections 3 and 5, which rises with faster mortality decline, and λ ≤ 1 is the share of growth that does fiscal work. The defaults:

  • Growth. Rapid: μ = 1.8, σ = 2.3, the survey’s conditional median and its 10th–90th percentile range. Moderate: μ = 1.1, σ = 0.8. Slow: μ = 0.3, σ = 0.4. The means are the survey’s conditional medians; the two smaller spreads are judgments. The three together average 0.9 points, just above the survey’s unconditional median of 0.8.
  • Mortality decline. 0.73, 0.9, and 1.5 percent a year in the slow, moderate, and rapid scenarios: the more capable the AI, the faster the medical progress.
  • Leaks. For Social Security, λ is the share of gains reaching taxable wages (0.9). For the federal budget, income that bypasses wages still raises half as much revenue, and 10 percent of the gain is lost to interest rates beyond those CBO already assumes.

Under the default inputs, the odds that AI growth alone stops the debt from rising are about 22 percent, or roughly one in five. The odds that it closes Social Security’s gap are about 4.7 percent, roughly one in twenty. Both fall further if faster medical progress comes along with the growth, or if the gains bypass wages. To reach even odds on the federal bar, you would need to put most of your weight on rapid AI progress; to reach even odds on Social Security, you would need rapid progress that also leaves lifespans close to the Trustees’ path.

The last row of Table 1 deserves emphasis. The 2032–34 deadlines arrive before any plausible AI dividend could accumulate. In 97.5 percent of the Trustees’ stochastic simulations, the combined funds run dry by 2039. Whatever AI does to growth, Congress faces a Social Security decision within six years.

7Conclusion

AI is the best fiscal news the United States is likely to get. Even the mainstream forecasts would shrink the gap: half a point of extra growth closes about a fifth of Social Security’s shortfall and about 40 percent of the federal one. But growing out of the problem requires a boom at the top end of expert expectations. It also requires that boom to last for decades, to pay off in wages rather than profits, and to leave interest rates and lifespans roughly where they were. Those conditions are unlikely to hold together. The boom that would deliver the growth is the same boom most likely to deliver the longevity.

The policy lesson follows from the asymmetry. Growth is uncertain and arrives late, while the deadlines are fixed and close. Three implications stand out:

  • Act on the near-term shortfalls now. Treat any AI dividend as a bonus that reduces later adjustments.
  • Index benefits to longevity. Because longevity is the channel through which AI’s own success raises costs, the most valuable reform links benefits to it. That could mean tying the retirement age or benefit duration to life expectancy, as Denmark and Sweden do. Henderson’s proposal of a fixed 20-year benefit window, to be drawn whenever the retiree chooses, does the same.
  • Broaden the tax base. If AI shifts income from wages to capital, a tax base built on payroll will need to follow it.

Hope for the boom, but legislate for the median.
